# Three ways Henar can be better at quotes than Jupiter

Measured 16 September 2026, after the routing work in
`COMPETITIVENESS_2026-09-16.md`. That document concluded the remaining gap
was access, not algorithm, and that the market-maker tier was "not an adapter
you can write". **The second half of that is wrong**, and the correction is
edge 2 below.

The premise of the earlier work was to be better at the same question Jupiter
answers: best price for this mint. Henar loses that question by 23 bps at
$10,000 and cannot win it with public AMM liquidity alone. All three edges
here come from answering a *different* question, or from pricing it
differently.

---

## Edge 1 — Best execution for a company, not for a mint

> **Not being built. Declined by the owner on 17 September 2026: Henar quotes
> per issuer.** The measurement below is kept because it is real and may
> matter later, but the product answers "best price for this issuer's token",
> not "best exposure to this company". Nothing in the router should silently
> reach across issuers.

Henar's registry is companies → representations. Most listed companies have
three tokens: xStocks, Backpack and Ondo. Each is a claim on the same shares.
**Every other aggregator routes mint to mint and has no idea they are
related.**

So "how much Apple can I get for $1,000" is a strictly larger search than
"how much AAPLx can I get for $1,000", and only Henar has the mapping to ask
it.

Measured through Henar's own production quote path, $1,000 buys, 12 companies
with two or more priced representations:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Median gain from taking the better issuer | **20 bps** |
| Above 5 bps | 12 of 12 |
| Above 25 bps | 5 of 12 |
| Above 100 bps | 2 of 12 |
| Largest | 2,347 bps (NVO) |

**The median cross-issuer gain is larger than Henar's entire routing deficit
to Jupiter.** Examples: UPS 121 bps better on Backpack than xStocks; LLY
54 bps; DELL 49 bps; WEN 71 bps. The winning issuer was Ondo 7 times,
Backpack 4, xStocks 1 — no issuer is reliably best, which is why this has to
be measured per quote rather than configured.

It does not need to be predictable. This is a live comparison at quote time,
not a forecast, so a spread that flips between issuers is just as capturable
as one that persists.

### What makes it honest

The product rule is that representations are never silently substituted, and
that stands. This is an offer, not a swap: *"You asked for AAPLx. AAPLon
gets you 0.4% more Apple for the same dollars. Switch?"* — with the issuer,
its redemption terms and its token extensions named.

### What it needs first

The comparison assumes one token is one share. xStocks publishes a
redemption rate per company precisely because that can drift, and Henar
already maps 17 of those Pyth feeds. The current demo key cannot read them
(`NOT_ENTITLED`). **A wider Pyth entitlement is a prerequisite for shipping
this**, not a nice-to-have: without it the tight 8–50 bps cases are fine but
an outlier like NVO's 2,347 bps cannot be told apart from a unit mismatch.

## Edge 2 — Integrate the market makers by simulation, not by SDK

The previous document said HumidiFi, ZeroFi, BisonFi and the rest were
"permissioned market makers that quote off-chain" and "not an adapter anyone
can write". That was an assumption, and it is false. Their pools are ordinary
Solana accounts under ordinary programs:

| Venue | Program | Pool account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HumidiFi | `9H6tua7jkLhdm3w8BvgpTn5LZNU7g4ZynDmCiNN3q6Rp` | 1,728 bytes |
| ZeroFi | `ZERor4xhbUycZ6gb9ntrhqscUcZmAbQDjEAtCf4hbZY` | 38,178 bytes |
| GoonFi V2 | `goonuddtQRrWqqn5nFyczVKaie28f3kDkHWkHtURSLE` | 2,049 bytes |
| BisonFi | `BiSoNHVpsVZW2F7rx2eQ59yQwKxzU5NvBcmKshCSUypi` | 2,049 bytes |

Nothing is permissioned. They are closed-source, which is a different
problem: there is no SDK and no published curve to reimplement. Every
aggregator integrates a venue by reimplementing its math, which is exactly
what a closed-source program blocks.

**Henar does not have to reimplement anything.** It already runs a mandatory
pre-wallet simulation on every route. Simulating a swap instruction returns
the exact output the program would produce, whatever its math is. Promoting
simulation from *verification* to *price discovery* turns every on-chain
venue into an integrable one:

1. Find the venue's pools by mint, the way Meteora discovery already does.
2. Learn the instruction layout by decoding Jupiter's own landed transactions
   through that program — public data on chain.
3. Price by `simulateTransaction`, not by a reimplemented curve.

The cost is one simulation per venue per quote, which is why aggregators
reimplement math instead. Henar does not have to pay it on every keystroke:
the indicative estimate can stay fast and cheap, and simulation can price the
final quote, where the basis points are actually spent.

This is the only path to the liquidity that beats Henar today, and it needs
no relationship with anyone.

---

## Edge 3 — Charge only when Henar wins

10 bps of the 11–19 bps user-facing gap is Henar's own fee, charged on a
resold Jupiter route the user could have had from Jupiter for nothing. No
routing work can ever fix that: reselling a route and adding a fee is losing
by the fee, by construction.

Henar already computes the public benchmark on every quote — Jupiter's number
sits in `alternatives` on every request. So the fee can be a share of
measured outperformance rather than a fixed rate:

- Henar's route ties or loses to the benchmark → **fee is zero**, and the
  user is never worse off for having come here.
- Henar's route beats it by 40 bps → take a fifth, 8 bps, and the user still
  keeps 32 bps they could not have got elsewhere.

That makes "Henar is never worse than going direct" a true statement rather
than a marketing one, and it makes every basis point of routing work directly
revenue-generating instead of merely closing a self-inflicted gap. It is a
pricing decision, not an engineering one, but the measurement it depends on
already exists.

---

## Order

Edge 1 is declined. Edge 3 is a configuration change and an afternoon of
work. Edge 2 is the biggest piece of engineering and the only one that
reaches the liquidity Henar currently cannot see.

# The unexplained 9 bps had a cause, and it is fixed

The owner asked on 17 September why the user-facing gap was 19 bps when
Henar's own fee is 10. The answer was not routing.

Jupiter answers the same question at two endpoints. `/swap/v2/order` reaches
JupiterZ, its RFQ network; `/swap/v1/quote` does not. Henar asked only the
first. On thin names the RFQ endpoint is worth a great deal — 262 bps on AMC,
102 bps on IBM — but on liquid names, where both find the same route, the
plain quote came back about 10 bps better, in 7 of 14 pairs and almost
exactly 10 bps each time.

So on every liquid pair Henar resold Jupiter 10 bps below Jupiter's own price
and charged 10 bps on top. Henar could not have seen it: the adapter recorded
Jupiter's provider fee as `0` without ever reading the field.

It now asks both and keeps the better answer.

| Residual after removing Henar's own fee | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Median | **-10.0 bps** | **+0.7 bps** |
| Pairs worse than -2 bps | 7 of 14 | **0 of 12** |

And the user-facing gap, same harness as the rest of this document:

| Notional | Start of work | After routing work | After the endpoint race |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1,000 | -13.0 bps | -11.4 bps | **-10.0 bps** |
| $10,000 | -18.2 bps | -18.7 bps | **-9.7 bps** |

The gap is now Henar's fee and nothing else, tightly clustered: at $1,000 the
worst of 14 observations was -10.6 bps. The RFQ upside is kept, so thin names
still win by 267 bps where Henar's Jupiter access beats the plain quote.

Which leaves Edge 3 as the whole remaining gap. Every basis point a user
loses by coming to Henar is now the fee, deliberately charged, and nothing
accidental.
